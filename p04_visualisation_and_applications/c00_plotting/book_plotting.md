## Plotting with Plotly
This chapter first summarises a few principles of good charts and then shows how to implement them
with Plotly. All examples use datasets that ship with Plotly (`px.data.tips()`, `gapminder()`,
`iris()`, `stocks()`), so no download is needed.

### Principles of good charts
**Start with the question, then pick the form.** The data's job determines the chart type:

| job | typical form |
|---|---|
| distribution of one variable | histogram, box plot, violin, ECDF |
| comparison of groups / ranking | (sorted) bar chart, dot plot |
| relationship of two variables | scatter plot (log axis for skewed data) |
| change over time | line chart (area if the total matters) |
| many variables at once | scatter matrix, correlation heatmap, small multiples |
| a single headline number | no chart - a KPI tile with the change versus a reference |

**Reduce, then emphasise.** Remove everything that does not carry information (heavy grid lines,
borders, 3D effects, decorative colours) and direct attention to what matters: a descriptive title that
states the finding ("Dinner bills are higher ..."), a highlighted series with the rest in grey, a
reference line, a direct label at the end of a line.

**Use colour for one job at a time.** Colour can encode *identity* (categorical: one fixed hue per
series, always in the same order, so a series keeps its colour in every chart), *magnitude*
(sequential: one hue from light to dark) or *polarity* (diverging: two opposite hues with a neutral
grey midpoint, e.g. for correlations). Rainbow scales distort magnitudes, and more than about seven
colours cannot be told apart - fold the rest into "other" or use small multiples. About 8 % of men have
a colour vision deficiency, so palettes should be checked for it and colour should never be the only
carrier of meaning (add labels, legends, line styles or position).

**Do not mislead.** Bar charts start at zero (the length encodes the value); time axes are evenly
spaced; avoid dual y-axes - the alignment of two scales is arbitrary and suggests correlations that
are not in the data. Use two stacked panels with a shared x-axis, or index both series to a common base.

**Make it accessible and reproducible.** Readable font sizes, sufficient contrast, the underlying
numbers available in a table or tooltip, and charts generated by code rather than by hand.

`utils/style.py` turns these principles into a Plotly template (`apply_style()`): a fixed categorical
palette of 8 colours whose *adjacent* slots were checked for colour vision deficiencies (some
non-adjacent pairs, e.g. orange and dark green, look alike to colour-blind readers, so a chart with many
series should pick slots that are all distinct and label the lines directly), a single-hue sequential
and a blue-grey-red diverging scale, hairline
grid lines, muted axis labels and thin marks. Registering a template once makes every figure of a
project consistent.

### Plotly Express (`main_1_plotly_express.py`)
`plotly.express` maps columns of a tidy data frame to visual channels (`x`, `y`, `color`, `size`,
`facet_col`, `animation_frame`, `hover_name`, ...) and returns a complete figure:

```python
fig = px.histogram(tips, x="total_bill", color="time", marginal="box", nbins=30, barmode="overlay")
fig.write_html("outputs/histogram.html")
```

The script creates

1. a histogram with a marginal box plot (distribution of bills for lunch vs. dinner),
2. box plots per weekday with all points shown (small datasets deserve their raw points),
3. a scatter plot of GDP per capita (log scale) vs. life expectancy where one continent is highlighted
   and all others are grey - instead of five colours competing for attention,
4. small multiples: one panel per continent, one thin line per country (`facet_col`, `line_group`),
5. a sorted horizontal bar chart of the top 15 countries by GDP per capita, in one colour,
6. a scatter matrix of the iris measurements (three species, three colours),
7. the classic Gapminder animation (`animation_frame="year"`). The axis ranges are fixed for all frames
   (`range_x`, `range_y`) - otherwise the axes would jump - and must therefore cover the extremes of
   *all* years, such as Kuwait's GDP in the 1950s and Rwanda's life expectancy in 1992. The HTML files
   are written with `auto_play=False`, so the animation starts when the reader presses play.

Each figure is saved as an interactive HTML file in `outputs/`. The individual files load plotly.js from
a CDN (small files, need internet to view), while `save_report` combines all figures into one page that
embeds plotly.js once and works offline.

### Graph objects (`main_2_graph_objects.py`)
Behind every Plotly figure is a JSON-like structure of *traces* (data + mark type) and a *layout*.
`plotly.graph_objects` builds it explicitly, which is needed for fine control:

- **Time series with range slider**: one `go.Scatter` per ticker (prices indexed to 100), a range slider
  and range selector buttons (3M, 6M, YTD, All) on the x-axis, a unified hover that shows all series
  at the hovered date, and direct labels at the line ends (`add_annotation`), nudged apart where two
  lines end close together. The six colours are palette slots that all remain distinguishable for
  colour-blind readers.
- **Correlation heatmap** of weekly returns: `go.Heatmap` with the diverging scale fixed to $[-1, 1]$
  (`zmin=-1, zmax=1`), so that its neutral grey midpoint really means "no correlation", plus the
  values as text (`texttemplate`). (`zmid` only centres an automatically chosen range; Plotly ignores
  it when `zmin` and `zmax` are given.)
- **Subplots** with `make_subplots(rows=2, shared_xaxes=True)`: the price of one stock and its drawdown
  from the running maximum, $\mathrm{DD}_t = P_t / \max_{s \le t} P_s - 1$, in two panels instead of
  one chart with two y-axes. A shaded rectangle (`add_vrect`) marks the period from the peak to the
  trough, an annotation labels the maximum drawdown.
- **Export**: `write_html` (interactive), `pio.to_json`/`pio.from_json` (to store or transfer a figure)
  and `write_image` for static PNG/SVG/PDF files, which requires the optional `kaleido` package and a
  Chrome/Chromium browser.

Plotly figures can be shown in notebooks (`fig.show()`), saved as HTML, embedded in web frameworks or
used in dashboards - as in the next chapter.
